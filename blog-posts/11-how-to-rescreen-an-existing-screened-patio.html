Rescreening is the single most common job we do, and it's also the one homeowners understand the least. The good news is simple: if your frame is solid, you almost never need a new enclosure — you need new screen. Here's how rescreening actually works and what to expect.

What rescreening really means

Rescreening replaces the mesh on your existing aluminum frame. We strip out the old screen and the rubber spline that holds it, clean the frame channels, roll in fresh mesh pulled tight, and seat new spline. The structure you already have stays put. You're paying for new screen and labor, not a rebuild — which is why it costs a fraction of a new cage.

When it's time to rescreen

  • The mesh is grey, chalky, and brittle from sun-rot.
  • Panels are torn, sagging, or pulling out of the frame.
  • Bugs are getting through even though the frame looks fine.
  • You're selling and want the whole enclosure to look new again.

One panel or the whole thing?

You don't always have to do everything. If a branch tore one or two panels and the rest is fine, we replace just those. But if the screen is at the end of its life across the enclosure, a full rescreen makes more sense than chasing one failing panel after another. I'll tell you honestly which situation you're in.

Can you DIY it?

A single panel or a screen door is a reasonable weekend project if you're handy — a spline roller and a roll of mesh will do it. Where DIY tends to go wrong is tension: under-tension the screen and it sags and waves within months. A full enclosure, especially a tall one, is a lot of fiddly, repetitive work on a ladder, which is where most people decide it's worth a crew.

Pick the right mesh while you're at it

A rescreen is the perfect time to upgrade. Moving from standard to no-see-um mesh, or adding pet-resistant screen on the lower panels, costs little extra when we're already in there — and it fixes the exact problems that made you look at the screen in the first place.

My honest take

Don't let anyone sell you a new enclosure when a rescreen will do. Make them show you the frame is actually bad before you spend on a rebuild. I've rescreened the same cage three and four times over the years on its original frame.
