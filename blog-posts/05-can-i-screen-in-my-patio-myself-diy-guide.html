I'll be honest in a way that might surprise you coming from a screen company: some patio screen work really is DIY-friendly, and I'll tell you which parts. I'll also tell you where I've watched well-meaning homeowners get in over their heads and call us to fix it — which usually costs more than doing it right from the start.

What you can reasonably do yourself

If you're handy and patient, a few jobs are genuinely doable on a weekend:

  • Rescreening a single panel or a screen door. With a spline roller, a utility knife, and a roll of mesh, you can replace torn screen in an existing frame. It's fiddly but learnable.
  • Cleaning and minor spline fixes. Pressing loose spline back in, clearing debris, tightening hardware.
  • Replacing a screen door roller or handle.

For these, your frame is already up and holding the load. You're just maintaining it.

What you should not DIY

Building a new enclosure is a different animal. Here's where I'd pump the brakes:

  • A full new screen enclosure. It has to be engineered and permitted to Florida wind-load code. Get the engineering or anchoring wrong and it can fail in a storm — or fail inspection and have to come down.
  • Anything structural — beams, uprights, attaching to the house, super gutters.
  • Tall or large spans. Big panels fight back. Getting mesh tight and square on a large opening without it sagging is harder than it looks, and the ladder work is genuinely dangerous.

Where DIY usually goes wrong

The most common call I get from a DIY job is baggy, wavy screen. People under-tension the mesh or let the spline pop, and within a few months it sags and bugs get in. The second most common is a door that never sits square again. Neither is the end of the world, but by the time we re-do it, they've spent on materials twice.

My honest take

If you've got one torn panel, buy the spline and the mesh and give it a shot — worst case you call us. But if you're screening in a whole patio or building a cage, that's not the project to learn on. The permit, the engineering, and the storm safety are worth a pro. And frankly, the labor to rescreen a whole enclosure isn't where most of the cost is anyway — the materials and the code work are.
