This question usually comes from homeowners who want that clean, open view with nothing blocking it — a lake, a golf course, the pool. It's a great goal, and modern aluminum makes big open spans possible. But there are real limits, and this is one place where I won't cut corners, because it's a storm-safety issue.

The honest answer: it depends on engineering

There's no single magic width, because it's not about the screen — it's about the beam. The wider you go without a support post, the bigger and stronger the structural beam has to be to carry the load and meet Florida wind-load code. Engineering decides the number, not a rule of thumb. A properly sized beam can clear a wide opening; an undersized one fails, and in a hurricane zone that's not a risk worth taking.

Picture windows and panoramic spans

The "no cross-bars" look people love is a panoramic or picture-window build. We remove the mid-height bars on the view side and use heavier uprights and beams to carry the span. It costs more precisely because of that engineering — the strength to open up the view safely is where the money goes. When someone offers you a huge open span cheap, be suspicious of what's holding it up.

When you need a support post

Past a certain width, a support post isn't a failure — it's the right engineering call. I'd rather give you a clean build with one well-placed post than a postless span that bends or fails in a storm. Often we can position that post so it barely interrupts the view, at the corner of a sightline rather than dead center.

Best practices for large openings

  • Get it engineered for your specific wind zone — Miami-Dade's HVHZ standard is stricter than inland Broward.
  • Use heavier beams and uprights, not standard ones stretched past their rating.
  • Don't chase the widest possible span if a post gives you a stronger build and barely changes the view.
  • Hire licensed — a big open span is exactly the kind of build where unlicensed shortcuts show up later.
